When phones, cell networks or GPS stop working, you may need to find your way the old way. This guide covers maps, compasses, natural direction finding and leaving clear notes for people following you.

## Before you set out

Moving is not always safer than staying put. Before you leave, ask whether you really need to go.

- **Tell someone** where you are going, your route and when you expect to arrive. If no one is home, leave a note. See the trail notes section below.
- **Note the time** you leave, and keep track of how long you have been walking.
- Look at the map and plan your route before you start, including landmarks, water, shelters and places where you could turn back.
- Carry water, a flashlight, a whistle, warm clothing, a map and a compass if you have them.
- Plan to arrive before dark. Navigation is much harder at night.

### If you become lost

Many search and rescue groups teach **STOP**: **S**top, **T**hink, **O**bserve, **P**lan. Stay calm and do not keep walking aimlessly. If others know where you were going, it is often best to stay where you are, find shelter and signal for help. Three whistle blasts is a recognised distress signal.

## Using a paper map

Keep printed road maps of your area and region in your emergency kit, since phone maps may not load. Mark important places in advance: hospitals, shelters, water sources, family meeting points and evacuation routes.

### Reading a map

- **Legend:** explains the symbols for roads, rivers, buildings and other features.
- **Scale:** shows how distance on the map relates to distance on the ground, for example 1 inch to 1 mile, or 1 cm to 1 km.
- **North:** most maps have north at the top. Check the arrow.
- **Contour lines** on topographic maps join points of equal height. Lines close together mean steep ground. Lines far apart mean gentle slopes.

### Orienting the map

Turn the map so features on it line up with what you see around you, such as a road, river or hill. Once the map is lined up, directions on the map match directions on the ground. Keep re-checking it as you move.

## Compass basics

A magnetic compass needle points to **magnetic north**, which is usually a little different from true north on a map.

- Hold the compass **flat and level** in front of you.
- Keep it away from metal, cars, power lines and electronics, which can pull the needle off.
- The coloured end of the needle, usually red, points north.

### Following a direction (bearing)

1. Turn the compass dial until the bearing you want, in degrees, lines up with the direction-of-travel arrow or index line.
2. Holding the compass flat, turn your whole body until the red end of the needle sits inside the north outline arrow on the dial.
3. Look along the travel arrow and pick a landmark in that direction, such as a tree or building.
4. Walk to it, then repeat with a new landmark.

### Declination

The difference between magnetic north and true north is called **declination**. It varies by location and changes slowly over time. Many topographic maps print it in the margin. Over long distances, adjust for it, or you will drift off course.

## Using the sun

The sun gives a rough direction anywhere in the world.

- The sun **rises in the east and sets in the west**. This is only exact around the spring and autumn equinoxes. In summer it rises and sets further toward the pole you are nearest to.
- Around midday, in most of the **northern hemisphere**, the sun is in the **south**.
- Around midday, in most of the **southern hemisphere**, the sun is in the **north**.
- Near the equator, the midday sun can be almost directly overhead, so this is less reliable.

### The shadow-stick method

This works in both hemispheres on a sunny day.

1. Push a straight stick upright into flat ground.
2. Mark the tip of its shadow with a stone.
3. Wait about **15 minutes**. Mark the new tip of the shadow.
4. Draw a line from the first mark to the second. This line runs roughly **west to east**. The first mark is west.
5. Stand with the first mark on your left and the second on your right. You are now facing roughly north.

## Using the stars

On a clear night, stars can show direction more precisely than the sun.

### Northern hemisphere: the North Star

The North Star (**Polaris**) sits almost directly over the North Pole, so it marks true north.

1. Find the **Big Dipper**, a group of seven bright stars shaped like a ladle or saucepan.
2. Find the two stars that form the outer edge of the cup, furthest from the handle. These are the **pointer stars**.
3. Follow the line through them, away from the bottom of the cup, for about **five times** the distance between them.
4. You reach a moderately bright star on its own. That is Polaris. Face it and you are facing north.

### Southern hemisphere: the Southern Cross

1. Find the **Southern Cross**, four bright stars in a kite-shaped cross. Two bright **pointer stars** are nearby, which helps you tell it from a similar, dimmer "false cross".
2. Imagine a line through the long axis of the cross, from top to foot.
3. Extend that line beyond the foot about **four and a half times** the length of the cross.
4. From that point, look straight down to the horizon. That direction is roughly **south**.

## Landmarks and staying on track

Landmarks are often the easiest way to navigate, especially in towns.

- **Use handrails:** follow long features such as roads, rivers, railway lines, power lines or coastlines that lead toward where you want to go.
- **Note features at every turn,** such as a church, water tower, bridge or unusual building.
- **Look back often.** The route looks different on the way home. Remember what it looks like from the other direction.
- **Count paces or time.** Knowing roughly how far you have walked helps you find your place on a map. Many people walk about 3 miles (5 km) per hour on flat roads, much less on rough ground.
- Use high ground to look around and spot landmarks, if it is safe.
- In towns, street numbers often rise in a steady direction, which helps you keep track.

Do not rely on myths such as moss growing only on one side of trees. They are not reliable.

## Leaving a trail of notes

Notes help family and rescuers find you, and help you find your way back.

### What to write

- Names of who is with you
- **Date and time**
- **Where you are going** and the route you plan to take
- Your condition and supplies, for example "all well, have water"
- When you expect to arrive or return

### Where to leave notes

- At home, on the door or inside a window. Avoid making it obvious to strangers that the home is empty.
- At meeting points, shelters and junctions where you change direction.
- At about eye level, protected from rain in a plastic bag, and held down firmly.

### Marking a route

- Use an **arrow** to show which way you went at each junction.
- Agree on simple marks with your group in advance, such as tape, chalk or stacked stones.
- Mark every turn, not just straight sections.
