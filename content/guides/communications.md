In a long outage, phone networks can be overloaded or down, and the internet may not work. This guide covers practical ways to get news, stay connected with family and call for help.

## Text instead of calling

When networks are busy, **text messages often get through when calls do not.** Texts use less network capacity and keep trying to send.

- **Text first.** Keep messages short and clear: who you are, where you are, whether you are safe, and what you need.
- **Keep calls short.** Say what you need to say and hang up so others can get through.
- If a call fails, wait about 10 seconds before redialling to reduce network congestion.
- **Emergencies:** Call 911 (US and Canada), 112 (Europe and many other countries) or 999 (UK). In many US areas you can **text 911** if you cannot call. If texting is not available, you will get a reply telling you to call.
- Update your voicemail greeting or social media status with a short "We are safe" message when you can, so people stop trying to reach you.

## Out-of-area contact and family plan

Local lines may be jammed while long-distance connections still work.

- Choose one **out-of-area contact**, a friend or relative in another city or region. Everyone in the family checks in with that person.
- Make sure everyone knows the contact's phone number **by heart** or has it written on paper in their wallet or bag. Phones die.
- Agree in advance what each person will do if separated: who picks up the children, where to meet, and how to check in.
- Children should know a trusted adult's phone number and their home address.
- Keep a written list of important numbers: family, doctors, pharmacy, utility companies, insurance, neighbours.

Store the out-of-area contact in your phone as **"ICE"** (In Case of Emergency) so responders can find it.

## Save your phone battery

A phone may be your lifeline for days. Make every percent count.

- Turn on **low power mode** or battery saver.
- **Lower screen brightness** and shorten the screen timeout.
- **Close apps** you are not using, especially games, video, maps and streaming.
- Turn off Bluetooth, Wi-Fi and location services when you do not need them.
- Use **airplane mode** when you do not need the phone. Searching for a weak signal drains the battery fast. Turn it off at set times to check messages.
- Avoid video calls and streaming.
- Keep the phone warm in cold weather, such as in an inside pocket. Cold drains batteries faster.
- If you have more than one phone in the household, keep one switched off as a spare.

Charge power banks and phones whenever power is available, such as at a warming centre or shelter.

## Charging from a car safely

A car can charge phones and power banks, but running engines produce **carbon monoxide**, a deadly gas.

- **Only run the engine outdoors**, in open air.
- **Never** run a car in a garage, even with the garage door open.
- Make sure the exhaust pipe is not blocked by snow, mud or debris before starting the engine.
- Do not sit in a running car for long periods in a closed or snowed-in space.
- Use a car charger or USB port. A power inverter can run small devices, but follow its instructions.
- Run the engine while charging so you do not drain the car battery, and keep some fuel in reserve in case you need to drive somewhere.

> **Warning:** If you feel dizzy, sick or headachy in or near a running car, turn off the engine and get into fresh air immediately.

## Radio: your most reliable news source

When the internet and phones are down, **radio still works.** A battery-powered or hand-crank radio is one of the most useful items you can own.

- **Local AM and FM news stations** broadcast emergency updates, shelter locations and official instructions. AM signals often travel farther, especially at night.
- Write down the frequencies of your local emergency and news stations before you need them.
- Listen at set times, such as each hour, to save batteries.
- A hand-crank or solar radio can also charge a phone on some models.

### NOAA Weather Radio (US)

In the United States, **NOAA Weather Radio** broadcasts weather warnings and other hazard information 24 hours a day on seven frequencies between **162.400 and 162.550 MHz**.

- You need a radio with the weather band (often marked "WX").
- Many models can sound an alert automatically when a warning is issued for your area.

Outside the US, check which national or local stations carry emergency broadcasts in your area.

## Walkie-talkies and ham radio

Two-way radios work without phone networks or the internet. Range depends on terrain, buildings and antennas, and is usually much shorter than the maximum printed on the package.

### FRS and GMRS walkie-talkies (US)

- **FRS** (Family Radio Service) radios need **no licence** in the US. They are good for keeping in touch within a building, a neighbourhood or a group on foot.
- **GMRS** radios can use more power and reach farther, but require an FCC licence (no exam) in the US.
- Agree in advance on a **channel** and check-in times with family and neighbours.
- Rules differ by country. Check local rules for licence-free radios where you live.

### Amateur (ham) radio

- Ham radio can reach across a city, a region, or the world. Volunteer operators often help pass emergency messages when other systems fail.
- Transmitting normally requires a **licence**, which needs an exam.
- Anyone can **listen** with a suitable receiver.
- In the US, FCC rules allow any means of radio communication in a genuine emergency when there is an immediate threat to life or property and no other means is available.

## Distress signals: how to call for help without a phone

If you are trapped, lost or need help and cannot call, use signals that people recognise.

- **Signals in threes** mean distress: **three whistle blasts**, three shouts, three flashes of light, three knocks, or **three fires** in a triangle or a line.
- **SOS** in Morse code is three short, three long, three short: **... --- ...**. You can flash it with a flashlight or tap it on pipes or walls.
- A **whistle** carries farther than your voice and uses less energy. Keep one in your kit.
- If trapped in rubble, **tap on a pipe or wall** rather than shouting, to save your energy and avoid breathing dust.
- To signal aircraft, make large shapes or letters (such as SOS) on open ground using contrasting materials, or wave bright cloth.
- At night, use a flashlight. During the day, a mirror can flash sunlight toward rescuers.

## Notes on doors and meeting points

When phones do not work, paper still does.

### Leave a written note

If you leave home, leave a note on the door or in a window where family and responders will see it. Include:

- Who was in the house, and who left
- **Where you have gone** (shelter, friend's address, meeting point)
- The date and time you left
- How to reach you

Keep it simple. Do not write that the home is empty in a place strangers can easily read if you are worried about security; a note inside a window or with a trusted neighbour also works.

### Meeting points

Agree on meeting places with your household before you need them:

- **Near home:** a spot just outside, such as a neighbour's mailbox or a big tree, for sudden emergencies like fire.
- **In the neighbourhood:** a library, school or place of worship, in case you cannot return home.
- **Out of the area:** a relative's or friend's home, in case you must evacuate.

## Using the LASTSYNC group session

LASTSYNC can connect a group over **local Wi-Fi without the internet**. This works when people are within range of the same Wi-Fi network, such as in one home, a shelter or a nearby building.

**To start a session:**

1. A **laptop hosts** the group session. Keep it charged, or plugged into whatever power is available.
2. The host's screen shows a **QR code**.
3. Others **join by scanning the QR code** with their phone.

**Quick messages you can send:**

- **"I'm safe"** to let everyone know you are okay
- **"Meet here"** to share a meeting point
- **"I need help"** to ask the group for assistance

**Tips:**

- Agree on regular check-in times to save battery.
- The group session only reaches people connected to the same local network. It does not replace calling emergency services. If someone needs urgent medical help and any phone works, call or text emergency services.
- Keep messages short and clear, just as with texts.
