**This guide is not a substitute for first aid training.** If any phone works, call emergency services first: 911 in the US and Canada, 112 in Europe and many other countries, 999 in the UK. In many US areas you can text 911 if you cannot call.

> **Important:** These steps follow mainstream public guidance. Do what you can, stay calm, and keep trying to reach professional help.

## Check the scene and call for help

Before helping, make sure you will not become a second victim.

1. **Look for danger:** traffic, fire, downed power lines, smoke, gas smell, rising water, unstable structures. If it is not safe, do not go in. Call for help.
2. **Check the person:** Tap their shoulder and shout, "Are you okay?" Look for normal breathing for no more than 10 seconds.
3. **Call for help:** Call 911, 112 or 999 (or your local number). If you cannot call, try texting. Send someone else to call or find help if you can, and to bring an AED (defibrillator) if one is nearby.
4. **Care:** Treat the most life-threatening problems first. In order: not breathing normally, severe bleeding, then everything else.

If the person is unresponsive but breathing normally and you do not suspect a neck or back injury, roll them onto their side so their airway stays clear, and keep watching their breathing.

Wear gloves if you have them, or use a plastic bag as a barrier, and wash your hands afterwards.

## Severe bleeding

Severe bleeding can kill within minutes. Act fast.

1. **Apply firm, direct pressure** on the wound with a clean cloth, gauze or your gloved hands. Press hard and keep pressing. Do not lift it to check.
2. If blood soaks through, add more cloth on top and keep pressing.
3. **Pack deep wounds:** For a deep wound, especially in the neck, shoulder or groin where a tourniquet cannot go, stuff clean gauze or cloth firmly into the wound, then press hard on top for several minutes.
4. **Use a tourniquet** for life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg that pressure does not stop.

How to place a tourniquet:

- Place it **2 to 3 inches (5 to 7 cm) above the wound**, between the wound and the heart.
- **Do not place it on a joint** such as the elbow or knee. Go above the joint if needed.
- Tighten until the bleeding stops. It will hurt. That is expected.
- **Note the time** it was applied, and write it on the person or the tourniquet if you can.
- Do not loosen or remove it. Leave that to medical professionals.

If you have no commercial tourniquet, keep up firm direct pressure. Improvised tourniquets often fail.

Keep the person warm and lying down, and get them to medical care.

## Hands-only CPR for adults

If an adult or teenager suddenly collapses and is **not breathing normally** (or only gasping), start CPR straight away.

1. Call or text emergency services, or have someone else do it. Send someone for an AED if one is nearby.
2. Kneel beside the person, who should be on their back on a firm surface.
3. Put the heel of one hand on the **center of the chest**, with the other hand on top.
4. Keep your arms straight and your shoulders over your hands.
5. Push **hard and fast**: at least **2 inches (5 cm)** deep, at **100 to 120 compressions per minute**. That is about the beat of the song "Stayin' Alive".
6. Let the chest come all the way back up between pushes.
7. **Do not stop** until help arrives, an AED is ready to use, the person starts breathing normally, or you are too exhausted to continue. If someone else can help, swap every 2 minutes.

### Using an AED

Turn it on and follow the voice prompts. Attach the pads as shown in the pictures. Stand clear when it says to.

### Children, infants and drowning

For children, infants and people who have drowned, CPR with rescue breaths is recommended if you are trained. If you are not trained, chest compressions alone are far better than doing nothing.

## Choking

Signs of severe choking: the person cannot speak, cough or breathe, or clutches their throat. If they can cough forcefully, encourage them to keep coughing.

### Adults and children over 1 year

1. Ask, "Are you choking? Can I help?" Call for emergency help or have someone else call.
2. Give **5 back blows**: stand slightly behind, support their chest with one hand, lean them forward, and give 5 firm blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
3. Then give **5 abdominal thrusts**: stand behind them, wrap your arms around their waist, make a fist just above the belly button, grab it with your other hand, and pull sharply inward and upward 5 times.
4. Repeat back blows and abdominal thrusts until the object comes out, or the person becomes unresponsive.

This is the American Red Cross method. American Heart Association courses have traditionally taught abdominal thrusts on their own. Both approaches are accepted. Use the one you were trained in.

For a pregnant person or someone too large to reach around, give **chest thrusts** at the center of the breastbone instead of abdominal thrusts.

### Infants under 1 year

Give 5 back blows with the baby face-down along your forearm, then 5 chest thrusts with two fingers on the center of the chest. **Never** give abdominal thrusts to an infant.

If the person becomes unresponsive, lower them to the ground, call for help and start CPR. Look in the mouth and remove an object only if you can see it.

## Burns

**Cool the burn right away with cool running water for at least 10 minutes.**

- Remove rings, watches and tight clothing near the burn before it swells. Do not pull off clothing that is stuck to the skin.
- **Do not use ice**, iced water, butter, oil or toothpaste. These can make the injury worse.
- Do not break blisters.
- After cooling, **cover loosely** with a clean, dry, non-fluffy dressing or plastic wrap.
- Keep the person warm. Cooling a large area can cause hypothermia, especially in children.

**Get medical care** for burns that are:

- Large (bigger than the person's palm), deep, or charred, white or leathery
- On the **face, hands, feet, genitals**, or a major joint
- Caused by **chemicals** or **electricity**
- In a young child or an older adult, or with any trouble breathing

For **chemical burns**, brush off dry chemicals, then flush with plenty of running water. For **electrical burns**, make sure the power source is off before touching the person. Electrical injuries can cause hidden internal damage and heart problems.

## Carbon monoxide poisoning

Carbon monoxide (CO) is a poisonous gas you cannot see or smell. It comes from generators, grills, camp stoves, heaters, cars and other fuel-burning devices, especially when used indoors or in a garage.

**Symptoms** (CDC): headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach or vomiting, chest pain and confusion. Symptoms are often described as "flu-like". People who are asleep or have been drinking can die without waking up. If several people or pets in the same place feel ill at once, suspect CO.

**What to do:**

1. Get **everyone** out into fresh air immediately, including pets. Do not stop to look for the source.
2. Call emergency services from outside.
3. If someone is unresponsive and not breathing normally, start CPR.
4. Do not go back inside until emergency responders say it is safe.
5. Anyone with symptoms needs medical care, even if they feel better in fresh air.

> **Warning:** Never use a generator, grill, camp stove or charcoal indoors, in a garage, or near windows, doors or vents.

## Hypothermia and frostbite

### Hypothermia

Hypothermia is a dangerously low body temperature. It can happen indoors in a cold outage, especially to older adults, babies and anyone who is ill.

**Signs:** shivering, exhaustion or drowsiness, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech. Babies may have bright red, cold skin and very low energy. Severe hypothermia may stop the shivering.

**What to do:**

1. Call for emergency help.
2. Get the person into a warm place and remove wet clothing.
3. Warm the **center of the body first** (chest, neck, head and groin) using dry blankets, layers of clothing or skin-to-skin contact under blankets.
4. If they are alert, give warm, sweet drinks. **No alcohol.**
5. Handle them gently. Keep them wrapped, including the head and neck.
6. If they are not breathing normally, start CPR.

### Frostbite

**Signs:** numbness; skin that looks white, greyish-yellow or unusually firm or waxy. Fingers, toes, ears, nose and cheeks are most at risk.

- Get into a warm place. Avoid walking on frostbitten feet if you can.
- **Do not rub** the area or use snow.
- Rewarm in **warm, not hot**, water, comfortable to an unaffected hand, or with body heat (for example, fingers in the armpits).
- Do not use a heating pad, fire, stove or radiator. Numb skin burns easily.
- **Do not thaw** the area if there is a chance it could refreeze.
- Seek medical care. Hypothermia is more urgent: treat it first.

## Heat exhaustion and heat stroke

### Heat exhaustion

**Signs:** heavy sweating; cool, pale, clammy skin; fast, weak pulse; nausea or vomiting; muscle cramps; tiredness or weakness; dizziness; headache; fainting.

**What to do:**

- Move to a cooler place, ideally shaded or air-conditioned.
- Loosen clothing. Apply cool, wet cloths or take a cool bath.
- Sip water.
- Get medical help if they are vomiting, if symptoms get worse, or if they last longer than 1 hour.

### Heat stroke is a medical emergency

**Signs:** body temperature of **103°F (39.4°C) or higher**; hot, red, dry or damp skin; fast, strong pulse; headache; dizziness; nausea; **confusion**; losing consciousness.

**What to do:**

1. **Call emergency services.**
2. Move the person to a cooler place.
3. **Cool them aggressively and fast.** If possible, immerse the body up to the neck in cold water. Otherwise, soak them with cool water, apply cold wet cloths or ice packs to the neck, armpits and groin, and fan them.
4. Do not give drinks to someone who is confused or not fully awake.
5. Keep cooling until help arrives or they recover.

## Shock

Shock happens when the body is not getting enough blood flow. It can follow severe bleeding, burns, serious injury, dehydration or a severe allergic reaction. It can be life-threatening.

**Signs:**

- Pale, cool, clammy skin
- Fast, shallow breathing
- Fast, weak pulse
- Restlessness, confusion or anxiety
- Weakness, dizziness, nausea or thirst

**What to do:**

1. Call emergency services.
2. Treat the cause if you can, such as stopping severe bleeding.
3. Have the person lie down on their back.
4. Keep them warm with a blanket or coat, including underneath if they are on cold ground.
5. Do **not** give food or drink.
6. Reassure them and keep watching their breathing. If they stop breathing normally, start CPR.

If the person has a severe allergic reaction and carries an epinephrine auto-injector, help them use it.

## Broken bones and splinting

Suspect a broken bone if there is pain, swelling, bruising, a deformed shape, or the person cannot move or put weight on the limb.

- **Do not try to straighten** the limb or push a bone back in.
- Support and **splint it in the position you found it.** Use padding plus something rigid, such as a board, rolled newspaper or magazine, or a stick.
- The splint should keep still the **joints above and below** the injury.
- Tie it in place firmly but not tightly. Check fingers or toes beyond the splint for warmth, colour and feeling. Loosen it if they become cold, pale, blue, numb or tingly.
- An arm can be supported with a sling made from a triangle of cloth.
- Apply a cold pack wrapped in cloth for up to 20 minutes at a time to ease swelling.
- If bone has come through the skin, do not push it back. Cover the wound with a clean dressing and control bleeding around it.

> **Warning:** If you suspect a head, neck or back injury, do not move the person unless they are in immediate danger. Keep the head and neck still.

## Cleaning wounds and signs of infection

In an outage, a small cut can become infected if it is not cleaned. Clean water matters: use bottled, boiled or disinfected water.

1. Wash your hands first, or wear gloves.
2. Stop the bleeding with gentle pressure.
3. Rinse the wound well with clean water. Wash the skin around it with soap.
4. Remove visible dirt or splinters with tweezers cleaned with alcohol.
5. Apply a thin layer of antibiotic ointment if you have it, and the person is not allergic.
6. Cover with a clean bandage. Change it daily, or whenever it becomes wet or dirty.

**Signs of infection:**

- Increasing redness, swelling, warmth or pain
- Pus or cloudy fluid
- Red streaks spreading from the wound
- Fever or chills

Seek medical care for these signs, for deep wounds, animal or human bites, puncture wounds, and wounds that may need stitches. Wounds exposed to floodwater or soil can cause tetanus. Ask a health worker about a tetanus shot if it has been more than 5 years since the last one.

## Dehydration and oral rehydration solution

Dehydration can be dangerous, especially for babies, young children and older adults, and after vomiting or diarrhoea.

**Signs:** thirst, dry mouth, little or dark urine, dizziness, tiredness. In babies: fewer wet diapers, no tears when crying, a sunken soft spot on the head, unusual sleepiness.

### Home oral rehydration solution (WHO-style recipe)

If you do not have packaged oral rehydration salts, mix:

- **1 litre (about 1 quart)** of clean, safe drinking water
- **6 level teaspoons** of sugar
- **1/2 level teaspoon** of salt

Stir until dissolved. Measure carefully. Too much salt can be harmful.

- Give small, frequent sips. If the person vomits, wait 10 minutes, then try again more slowly.
- Keep breastfeeding babies throughout.
- Make a fresh batch each day.

**Get medical help urgently** if the person is very sleepy or confused, cannot keep fluids down, has bloody diarrhoea, has not urinated in many hours, or is a baby with signs of dehydration.
