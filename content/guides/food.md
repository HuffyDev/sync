Food poisoning is the last thing you need during an emergency, when medical help may be hard to reach. This guide follows USDA food safety guidance on what to keep, what to throw out and how to eat safely.

## How long cold food stays safe

Keep the refrigerator and freezer doors **closed** as much as possible. With the doors closed, USDA says:

- A **refrigerator** keeps food safely cold for about **4 hours**.
- A **full freezer** holds its temperature for about **48 hours**.
- A **half-full freezer** holds its temperature for about **24 hours**.

Each time you open a door, these times get shorter. Plan meals so you open the fridge as few times as possible, and eat the most perishable food first.

To stretch the time:

- Group frozen items together in the freezer so they keep each other cold.
- Add bags of ice, frozen water bottles or block ice to the fridge.
- Dry ice can keep a freezer cold for longer. Handle it with gloves or tongs, never with bare hands, and keep the room ventilated.
- Coolers packed with ice are useful for milk, medicines and other items you need to reach often.

## The key rules: 40°F (4°C) and 2 hours

USDA's rule is simple: **throw out perishable food that has been above 40°F (4°C) for 2 hours or more.**

- Perishable means meat, poultry, fish, eggs, milk, soft cheese, leftovers and other foods that normally need refrigeration.
- **When in doubt, throw it out.**
- **Never taste food to decide whether it is safe.** Food can look and smell normal and still make you very ill. Some bacteria produce toxins that cooking does not destroy.

### Use an appliance thermometer

An appliance thermometer kept inside the fridge and freezer is the best way to know what happened while you were away or asleep.

- The fridge should be at **40°F (4°C) or below**.
- The freezer should be at **0°F (-18°C) or below**.
- If you have no appliance thermometer, a food thermometer can check individual items.

## What usually keeps and what to throw out

This applies to food that has been above 40°F (4°C) for **more than 2 hours**. If in doubt, throw it out.

### Usually safe to keep

- Hard cheeses such as cheddar and parmesan
- Butter and margarine
- Fresh whole fruits and raw vegetables
- Fruit juices
- Peanut butter, jelly, jam
- Ketchup, mustard, relish, pickles, soy sauce, and vinegar-based dressings
- Bread, rolls, cakes and muffins without cream or custard fillings

### Throw out

- Raw or cooked meat, poultry, fish and seafood, including deli meats and hot dogs
- Milk, cream, yogurt, and soft cheeses such as cottage cheese, ricotta and brie
- Eggs and egg dishes
- Leftovers and casseroles
- Cooked pasta, cooked rice and cooked vegetables
- Cut fresh fruit
- Cream-filled pastries and pies
- Opened baby formula

Throw out any food with an unusual smell, colour or texture, even if it was kept cold.

## Refreezing thawed food

You can safely **refreeze** food from the freezer if it:

- still contains **ice crystals**, or
- is at **40°F (4°C) or below** when you check it with a thermometer.

Refrozen food may lose some quality and texture, but it is safe.

If thawed food has been above 40°F (4°C) for more than 2 hours, do not refreeze or eat it. Throw it out.

Check each package separately. Items near the door or top of the freezer usually thaw first. Do not judge by how the outside of a package feels alone.

## Eating without cooking

Food that needs no cooking, little water and no refrigeration is the easiest to manage in an outage.

- Canned meat, fish, beans, fruit and vegetables (canned food can be eaten cold straight from the can)
- Peanut butter or other nut butters
- Crackers, bread, tortillas and rice cakes
- Granola, cereal and protein or energy bars
- Dried fruit, nuts and seeds
- Shelf-stable milk, plant milks and juice boxes
- Ready-to-eat soups that can be eaten at room temperature

Tips:

- Keep a **manual can opener** with your supplies.
- Avoid very salty foods if water is short, because they make you thirsty.
- Use the most perishable food from the fridge first, then the freezer, then your pantry.
- Remember special diets, infant food, and pet food.

## Cooking safely in an outage

Grills, camp stoves and charcoal produce carbon monoxide, a deadly gas you cannot see or smell.

- **Use grills, camp stoves and charcoal only outdoors**, away from windows, doors and vents.
- **Never** use them inside a home, garage, basement, tent or any partly enclosed space, even with windows open.
- Never use a gas oven or gas stove top to heat your home.
- Keep grills and stoves away from overhangs and anything that can burn. Keep children and pets back.
- Let charcoal cool completely before disposing of it.
- Keep a fire extinguisher or a bucket of water or sand nearby.

Cook meat and poultry thoroughly. A food thermometer helps you know food reached a safe temperature. Eat cooked food promptly, and do not keep cooked leftovers without refrigeration.

## Damaged cans and packages

Cans can be damaged during storms, floods or moves. USDA advises throwing out canned food if the can is:

- **Bulging** or swollen
- **Leaking**
- **Deeply dented**, especially on the top, bottom or side **seams**
- Badly rusted, with rust that does not wipe off
- Spurting liquid or foam, or the food smells bad, when you open it

Small dents that are not on a seam are usually fine.

> **Warning:** Do not taste food from a damaged can. Swollen or leaking cans can contain the toxin that causes botulism, which can be fatal.

## Food touched by floodwater

Floodwater carries sewage and chemicals. Throw out any food that may have touched floodwater, including:

- Food in cardboard, paper, plastic wrap or cloth
- Containers with screw caps, snap lids, pull tops or crimped caps, including bottled drinks
- Home-canned food
- Fresh produce
- Food from a fridge or freezer that was flooded

Undamaged, commercially made **all-metal cans** and **retort pouches** (such as foil pouches of tuna) can be saved:

1. Remove the labels.
2. Wash with soap and clean water, and rinse.
3. Sanitize by placing them in boiling water for 2 minutes, or in a solution of 1 cup (about 250 mL) of unscented household bleach in 5 gallons (about 19 litres) of water for 15 minutes.
4. Air dry, then relabel the contents and use-by date with a marker.

Wash and sanitize dishes, pots and utensils that touched floodwater before use.
