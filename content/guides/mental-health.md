Disasters affect the mind as well as the body. Feeling upset is a normal response to an abnormal situation, and most people recover with time and support. This guide covers simple ways to cope and to help others.

## Normal reactions to a disaster

After a disaster, people often feel:

- Anxious, fearful or on edge
- Sad, tearful or hopeless
- Angry or irritable
- Numb, or disconnected from what is happening
- Guilty, for example about surviving or not doing more
- Worried about the future

You may also notice:

- Trouble sleeping, or nightmares
- Trouble concentrating or making decisions
- Headaches, stomach problems or tiredness
- Changes in appetite
- Wanting to drink more alcohol or use other substances

These reactions are common, and they often ease over the following weeks. Strong feelings can come back around anniversaries or when new problems arise. Being kind to yourself and accepting support helps.

## Looking after yourself

- **Meet basic needs first:** water, food, rest and safety.
- **Keep some routine.** Regular times for waking, meals and sleep give structure to uncertain days.
- **Stay connected.** Talk to family, friends and neighbours. Sharing feelings and helping each other reduces stress.
- **Limit news and rumours.** Check for updates at set times instead of listening constantly.
- **Move your body.** A walk or simple stretches can ease tension.
- **Break problems into small steps.** Focus on what you can do today.
- **Avoid alcohol and drugs** as a way of coping. They can make sleep, mood and decision-making worse.
- Try slow breathing: breathe in gently through your nose for a count of four, then out slowly for a count of six. Repeat for a few minutes.
- Accept help when it is offered, and offer help to others if you can. Helping often makes people feel better too.

## Sleep

Good sleep makes it easier to cope, think clearly and stay healthy. Disasters often disturb it.

- Keep regular bedtimes and wake-up times when you can.
- Have a short wind-down routine, such as washing, reading or quiet talk.
- Make the sleeping area as dark, quiet and comfortable as possible.
- Avoid caffeine in the afternoon and evening, and avoid alcohol, which disturbs sleep.
- Try not to watch or listen to distressing news just before bed.
- If you cannot sleep, get up and do something quiet in low light until you feel sleepy.
- If worries keep you awake, write them down with one small step for tomorrow.

Children sleep better with familiar bedtime rituals and comfort objects, even in a shelter or a different home.

## Talking with children

Children take their cues from adults. Calm, honest and simple support helps them most.

- **Stay calm** and reassure them that adults are working to keep them safe.
- **Tell the truth simply**, in words that match their age. Do not give more detail than they need.
- **Listen.** Ask what they have heard and how they feel. Accept their feelings without judging.
- Answer questions briefly and honestly. It is fine to say you do not know.
- **Keep routines** for meals, play and bedtime as much as possible.
- **Limit exposure** to news and frightening adult conversations.
- Give them small, safe jobs, such as helping pack supplies. This gives a sense of control.
- Spend extra time together, with hugs, play and stories.

### Common reactions in children

Young children may become clingy, wet the bed again, have tantrums or sleep poorly. Older children may become withdrawn, irritable, have trouble at school or take risks. These often ease with time and support. Seek help if they last more than a few weeks or get worse.

## Psychological first aid basics

Psychological first aid means giving practical, caring support to someone in distress. You do not need to be a professional. WHO describes three actions: **Look, Listen and Link**.

### Look

Check that the area is safe. Look for people with urgent basic needs, such as injury, and for people showing severe distress.

### Listen

- Introduce yourself and approach calmly.
- Ask about their needs and concerns.
- Listen without pressuring them to talk about what happened.
- Help them feel calm. Speak gently and stay with them.

### Link

- Help them meet basic needs, such as water, food, shelter and medical care.
- Help them reach loved ones and available services.
- Give accurate information if you have it.

### What not to do

- Do not force anyone to share their story.
- Do not make promises you cannot keep.
- Do not judge what they did or did not do.
- Respect their right to make their own decisions.

Look after yourself too. Helpers need rest, food and support.

## When to seek help

Reach out to a health worker, counsellor, faith leader or crisis service if:

- Strong distress **does not ease after a few weeks**, or gets worse
- You cannot carry out daily tasks or care for yourself or others
- You are using alcohol or drugs to cope
- You keep reliving the event, or feel constantly on alert or numb
- Sleep problems continue for weeks

### Get help immediately

Get help right away if someone:

- Talks about wanting to die or harm themselves
- Talks about harming others
- Seems very confused or out of touch with reality
- Cannot keep themselves safe

Stay with them, remove anything they could use to hurt themselves if it is safe to do so, and get help.

### US crisis lines

- **Disaster Distress Helpline (SAMHSA):** call or text **1-800-985-5990**, free and available 24 hours a day.
- **988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline:** call or text **988**.

> **Warning:** If someone is in immediate danger, call 911 or your local emergency number if any phone works.

## Grief and loss

Disasters can bring the loss of loved ones, homes, pets, jobs and a sense of safety. Grief is a natural response to any of these losses.

- Everyone grieves differently and on their own timeline. There is no right way to feel.
- Grief can come in waves, including anger, guilt, numbness and deep sadness.
- Talk about the person or what was lost when you are ready. Share memories.
- Keep meaningful traditions or create small rituals of remembrance.
- Look after your health: eat, rest and stay connected to others.
- Children grieve too. Let them ask questions, include them in remembrance, and reassure them they will be cared for.

If grief makes it very hard to function for a long time, or if you feel hopeless, seek support from a health worker, counsellor or faith leader.
