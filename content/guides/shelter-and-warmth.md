Extreme cold and extreme heat are among the most dangerous parts of a long outage. Older adults, babies, people with health conditions and people living alone are at the greatest risk.

## Staying warm in a cold outage

Keep heat in, and keep people close together.

1. **Pick one room** to live in, ideally an interior room with few windows, on a lower floor if possible. Close doors to the rest of the house.
2. **Block drafts:** put towels or rags under doors and close curtains or blinds.
3. **Insulate windows:** cover them with blankets, plastic sheeting or cardboard at night. Open curtains on sunny windows during the day to let the sun warm the room, then close them at sundown.
4. **Dress in layers** of loose, warm clothing. Wool and synthetic fabrics stay warm when damp; cotton does not.
5. **Wear a hat, socks and gloves**, including in bed.
6. **Use blankets and sleeping bags.** Sharing a bed or sleeping area helps everyone stay warm.
7. **Eat regular meals and drink fluids.** Food gives your body the energy to make heat. Avoid alcohol, which makes you lose body heat faster.
8. **Keep dry.** Change out of wet clothes right away.

Check on older neighbours and people living alone. Watch everyone for signs of hypothermia: shivering, confusion, slurred speech, fumbling hands and drowsiness. See the First Aid guide.

## Heating safely: what never to use

Most deaths from home heating in outages come from **carbon monoxide** (CO) and **fire**.

**Never use these to heat your home:**

- A gas or electric **oven**, or a gas **stove top**
- **Grills**, charcoal or camp stoves
- A **generator**, even in a garage or with windows open
- A car running in an attached garage

These can fill the home with carbon monoxide, a gas you cannot see or smell that can kill within minutes, often while people sleep.

**Install battery-powered CO alarms**, or plug-in alarms with battery backup, near sleeping areas and on each level of the home. Test them now.

> **Warning:** If a CO alarm sounds or anyone feels dizzy, sick or headachy, get everyone outside into fresh air immediately and call for help from outside.

## Using fireplaces and fuel-burning heaters

If you use a fireplace, wood stove or kerosene heater, use it with care.

### Fireplaces and wood stoves

- Only use one that has been installed properly and kept clean. Ideally, have the chimney inspected yearly.
- Open the damper before lighting a fire and keep it open until the ashes are cold.
- Use a fireplace screen. Burn only dry wood. Never burn trash, cardboard or wrapping paper.
- Put cooled ashes in a covered metal container, outdoors, away from the house.

### Kerosene and other fuel-burning space heaters

- Use only heaters made for indoor use, and follow the instructions exactly.
- Use only the correct fuel. Never use gasoline in a kerosene heater.
- Turn the heater off and let it cool, then **refuel it outdoors**.
- **Ventilate:** open a window slightly while it runs to let fresh air in.
- Keep it at least **3 feet (1 metre) away** from anything that can burn, such as curtains, bedding, furniture and clothing.
- Never leave it running while you sleep or leave the room.
- Have a working **CO alarm** and **smoke alarm** in the same area.

## Keeping pipes from freezing

Frozen pipes can burst and flood your home. They are most at risk in unheated areas, along outside walls, and in crawl spaces, attics and garages.

**Before and during a freeze:**

- **Let cold water drip** from faucets served by exposed pipes. Moving water is less likely to freeze.
- **Open kitchen and bathroom cabinet doors** so warmer room air can reach the pipes.
- Keep garage doors closed if there are water pipes in the garage.
- Know where your **main water shut-off valve** is, and how to close it.

**If a pipe has frozen** (faucets give only a trickle or nothing):

- Keep the faucet open. As the pipe thaws, water will begin to flow.
- Warm the pipe gently with warm towels or warm water.
- **Never use an open flame**, torch or charcoal to thaw pipes.

**If a pipe bursts:**

1. **Shut off the main water valve** right away.
2. Move valuables and electrical items away from the water.
3. Do not touch electrical outlets or switches if you are standing in water.
4. Call a plumber when you can.

## Staying cool in a heat wave without air conditioning

Heat can be deadly, especially for older adults, babies and young children, people with health conditions, and people who live alone.

- **Stay on the lowest floor.** Heat rises, so basements and ground floors are coolest.
- **Block the sun:** close curtains and blinds on sunny windows during the day. Open windows at night if it is cooler and safe to do so.
- **Use water to cool down:** take cool showers or baths, or put wet cloths or towels on your skin, especially the neck, wrists and armpits.
- **Drink water regularly**, even if you do not feel thirsty. Avoid alcohol and very sugary drinks.
- **Wear light, loose, light-coloured clothing.**
- **Limit activity**, especially during the hottest part of the day. Rest in the shade.
- **Eat light meals** that need no cooking.
- Battery fans help, but CDC notes that in very high temperatures fans alone may not prevent heat illness. Cooling the skin with water works better.

> **Warning:** Never leave children, older adults or pets in a parked vehicle, even for a few minutes.

## Signs of heat illness

Check on people at risk at least twice a day, and watch for these signs.

**Heat exhaustion:** heavy sweating; cool, pale, clammy skin; fast, weak pulse; nausea or vomiting; muscle cramps; tiredness; dizziness; headache; fainting.

- Move to a cooler place, loosen clothes, apply cool wet cloths and sip water.
- Get medical help if symptoms get worse, last more than 1 hour, or the person is vomiting.

**Heat stroke is an emergency:** body temperature of 103°F (39.4°C) or higher; hot, red, dry or damp skin; fast, strong pulse; headache; dizziness; nausea; confusion; passing out.

- Call emergency services.
- Move the person somewhere cooler and cool them as quickly as possible with water, wet cloths, ice packs or cold water immersion.
- Do not give drinks to someone who is confused or not fully awake.

See the First Aid guide for more detail.

## Checking on people at higher risk

Some people cannot easily stay warm or cool on their own. Check on them in person if you can, at least once or twice a day.

- **Older adults**, especially those living alone
- **Infants and young children**, who lose heat quickly and overheat easily
- People with **heart, lung or kidney disease**, diabetes, or mental illness
- People who take medicines that affect body temperature or thirst
- People who use **powered medical equipment**
- People with limited mobility who cannot easily move to a safer place

Babies should sleep on their backs in a safe sleep space, even in the cold. Dress them in warm layers or a sleep sack rather than adding loose blankets, pillows or soft bedding.

## When to leave home

Your home is not always the safest place. Consider going to a warming centre, cooling centre, shelter, or the home of friends or family if:

- You cannot keep your living area warm enough, or cool enough, safely.
- Anyone shows signs of hypothermia, frostbite or heat illness.
- Someone depends on powered medical equipment or refrigerated medicine, and backup is running out.
- Your only heat source is unsafe, or your CO alarm has gone off.
- Water pipes have burst, or the home is damaged.
- Local officials tell you to leave.

Listen to a battery or hand-crank radio for the locations of warming and cooling centres and shelters. Take your emergency kit, medicines, phone chargers, important documents and warm clothing. Leave a note on the door saying where you have gone.

If you must travel in winter weather, tell someone your route and keep a kit in the car with blankets, water and snacks.

## Pets in extreme temperatures

Pets are at risk from cold and heat too.

- **Bring pets indoors.** Do not leave them outside or in a garage during extreme cold or heat.
- Keep them in your warm or cool room with you.
- Make sure they always have fresh, unfrozen water. They need more in hot weather.
- Check paws after walks in snow and ice for cuts, ice balls and road salt. Wipe them clean.
- In heat, walk dogs early or late in the day, and avoid hot pavement that can burn paws.
- Keep pets away from fuel-burning heaters, candles and antifreeze.

**If you leave home,** take your pets with you if it is safe. Many shelters accept only service animals, so ask ahead or listen for pet-friendly shelters on local radio. Take food, water, medicine, a leash or carrier, and your pet's records.
