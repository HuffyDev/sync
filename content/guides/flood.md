Floods are among the most common and deadly disasters, and most flood deaths happen in vehicles or when people walk into moving water. This guide covers what to do during a flood and how to stay safe afterwards.

## Turn around, don't drown

Never walk, swim or drive through floodwater. It is the single most important flood safety rule.

- Just **6 inches (15 cm) of moving water** can knock you down.
- About **12 inches (30 cm) of moving water** can carry away most cars.
- About **2 feet (60 cm) of moving water** can carry away larger vehicles, including SUVs and trucks.

You cannot tell how deep water is or how fast it is moving by looking. The road underneath may be washed away. Floodwater can also hide sharp debris, open drains and live electrical wires.

- Stay off bridges over fast-moving water.
- Be extra careful at night, when it is harder to see flooded roads.
- Find another route, or wait somewhere high and safe.

### If you are trapped in a vehicle

- If your vehicle is caught in fast-moving water, **stay inside**.
- If water is rising inside the vehicle, get out and onto the roof.

> **Warning:** Turn around, don't drown. Do not drive around barriers on a flooded road.

## Flood warnings and flash floods

- A **flood watch** means flooding is possible. Get ready to move.
- A **flood warning** means flooding is happening or will happen soon.
- A **flash flood warning** means sudden, dangerous flooding is happening or about to happen. **Move to higher ground immediately.** Do not wait for instructions.

Flash floods can arrive in minutes, sometimes far from where the rain fell. Low-lying areas, creeks, dry riverbeds, canyons, underpasses and areas below dams are most at risk.

Listen to a battery or hand-crank radio or NOAA Weather Radio for alerts. If you see water rising quickly or hear a roaring sound upstream, go to higher ground straight away.

## Evacuating

Leave when officials tell you to. Do not wait until water reaches your home.

1. Take your grab-and-go bag, medicines, phone, charger and important documents.
2. If you have time and it is safe, move valuables to an upper floor.
3. If told to, turn off electricity at the main breaker or fuse box, but **only if you are dry and not standing in water**.
4. Follow the evacuation routes officials give. Shortcuts may be flooded or blocked.
5. Take pets with you.

### If you are trapped in a building

- Go to the **highest level** of the building.
- **Do not climb into a closed attic.** You could be trapped by rising water.
- Go onto the roof only if necessary. Once there, signal for help.

## Electrical hazards

Water and electricity together can kill.

- **Stay away from downed power lines.** Assume every wire is live. Report it to the utility or emergency services.
- Do not walk through water that may be touching wires or electrical equipment.
- Do not enter a flooded basement or room if the power may still be on.
- Never touch electrical equipment, switches or outlets if they are wet or you are standing in water.
- If it is safe to reach the main breaker without standing in water, turn the power off. If not, leave it to the utility or an electrician.
- Have an electrician check any wiring, outlets or appliances that were flooded before using them.

> **Warning:** Never run a generator indoors, in a garage or near windows, doors or vents. Keep it at least 20 feet (6 metres) away from the home.

## Returning home safely

Go home only when officials say it is safe.

- Look for structural damage before going in, such as cracks, sagging or shifted foundations. Do not enter if the building looks unsafe.
- If you smell gas or hear a hissing sound, leave right away and report it from a safe distance.
- Wear rubber boots, waterproof gloves and long sleeves.
- Watch for snakes, other animals, broken glass and nails.
- Use flashlights, not candles or matches.
- Take photos of damage before cleaning, for insurance.

### Floodwater is dirty

Floodwater can contain sewage, chemicals and fuel. Keep children and pets out of it. Wash any skin that touches it with soap and clean water, and keep cuts covered. Wash clothes worn during clean-up in hot water and detergent, separate from other laundry.

## Food and drinking water after a flood

- **Throw away any food that may have touched floodwater**, including food in cardboard, plastic wrap, screw-cap or snap-lid containers, and home-canned food.
- Undamaged, commercially sealed metal cans and retort pouches can be saved if you remove the labels, wash and sanitize them. The Food guide explains how.
- Throw away perishable food if the power has been out too long. See the Food guide.
- Follow any **boil-water advisory** from local officials.
- If you have a private well that flooded, do not drink from it until it has been disinfected and tested.

When in doubt about any food or water, throw it out.

## Dealing with mold

Mold can start to grow within **24 to 48 hours** after flooding. It can cause breathing problems, especially for people with asthma, allergies or weak immune systems. They should stay out of the clean-up.

1. Open doors and windows. Use fans if you have power, but only if mold is not already growing.
2. Remove wet items that cannot be cleaned and dried quickly, such as carpet, mattresses, upholstered furniture, insulation and soaked drywall.
3. Clean hard surfaces with soap or detergent and water, then dry them completely.
4. Wear an **N95 respirator**, gloves and goggles while cleaning.

If you use bleach, CDC advises no more than **1 cup of bleach in 1 gallon of water** (about 60 ml per litre), with doors and windows open.

> **Warning:** Never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners. It makes toxic gas.

If mold covers more than about **10 square feet (about 1 square metre)**, EPA suggests getting professional advice.
