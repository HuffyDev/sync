Running out of an important medicine can turn an emergency into a crisis. This guide covers how to keep a supply, store medicines when power and refrigeration fail, and replace them when your usual pharmacy is closed.

## Keep a medication list

A written list helps any doctor, pharmacist or shelter worker help you quickly, even if your bottles are lost.

For each person, write down:

- Every medicine: **name, strength, dose and when it is taken**
- What each medicine is for
- Over-the-counter medicines, vitamins and supplements
- **Allergies** to medicines or foods
- Medical conditions
- Medical devices used, such as an oxygen concentrator or insulin pump, with model and supplier
- Doctor and pharmacy names and phone numbers
- Insurance details, if any

Keep one copy in your emergency kit, one in your wallet or bag, and one with an out-of-area contact. A photo of each prescription label on your phone is also useful. Update the list whenever a medicine changes.

## Keep an emergency supply

- Aim to keep **at least a 7-day supply** of each prescription medicine, and more if you can.
- Ask your doctor or pharmacist how to build up an extra supply. Some insurance plans allow early refills or longer supplies.
- **Rotate your stock.** Use the oldest medicine first and replace it with new, so your emergency supply does not expire.
- Keep medicines in their **original labelled containers**.
- Pack a few days' supply in your grab-and-go bag.
- Keep basic over-the-counter medicines too: pain and fever relievers, antihistamines, anti-diarrhoea medicine and oral rehydration salts.
- Keep spare supplies for medical equipment, such as syringes, test strips, inhaler spacers and batteries.

Store all medicines out of reach and sight of children.

## Storing medicines in heat or without a fridge

Most medicines should be kept at room temperature, usually about **68°F to 77°F (20°C to 25°C)**. Heat, humidity and sunlight can make them less effective.

- **Check the label or package insert** for each medicine's storage temperature. Ask a pharmacist if you are unsure.
- Store medicines in a cool, dry, shaded place. Do not leave them in a hot car or in direct sun.
- In a cooler, keep medicines in a sealed bag so they stay dry, and do not let them touch ice directly or freeze.
- Do not use medicine that has **changed in colour, smell or texture**, such as pills that are cracked, sticky or crumbly, or liquids that are cloudy when they should be clear.

### Medicines that need refrigeration

Some medicines, such as insulin, some liquid antibiotics and some injected medicines, need to be kept cold. Keep the fridge closed as much as possible during an outage. FDA advises that if a life-sustaining refrigerated medicine may have been damaged, you can keep using it until a replacement is available, then speak to a pharmacist or doctor as soon as you can.

> **Warning:** Medicines that have touched floodwater or dirty water should be replaced. See the section on damaged medicines below.

## Insulin and diabetes supplies

Insulin is less stable in heat, but it can be kept out of the fridge for a limited time.

- Many insulins can be kept at room temperature, between **59°F and 86°F (15°C and 30°C)**, for **up to about 28 days**, opened or unopened. Some products differ. **Check the package insert.**
- Keep insulin out of direct sunlight and away from heat.
- **Never freeze insulin.** Do not use insulin that has been frozen.
- Do not use insulin that looks unusual, such as clumped or discoloured, or cloudy when it should be clear.
- If you must use insulin that has been too warm, it may not work as well. Check blood sugar more often.

Keep together: insulin, syringes or pen needles, a glucose meter, test strips, lancets, extra batteries, ketone strips and a container for sharps.

### Low blood sugar

Keep **glucose tablets, juice or regular soda** close by. For low blood sugar, CDC advises the **15-15 rule**: take 15 grams of fast-acting sugar, check again after 15 minutes, and repeat if still low.

## Getting emergency refills

If you run out or lose your medicines:

- **Go to any open pharmacy** with your medication list, bottle or label. Pharmacies in the same chain can often see your records.
- In many US states, pharmacists can give an **emergency supply** of some medicines when your doctor cannot be reached, especially after a declared disaster.
- Ask staff at emergency shelters, the American Red Cross or a hospital. They can often help replace lost medicines, glasses and medical supplies.
- In some declared disasters, the US **Emergency Prescription Assistance Program (EPAP)** helps people without insurance replace lost prescriptions at participating pharmacies.

Do not take someone else's prescription medicine. A medicine that is safe for one person can be dangerous for another.

> **Warning:** Do not stop or reduce a needed medicine to make it last without talking to a doctor or pharmacist first. For some medicines this is dangerous.

## Medicines that may be damaged

Medicines can be damaged by heat, cold, moisture or contamination. When deciding whether to use them:

- **Floodwater:** Throw away any medicine that has come into contact with floodwater or dirty water, including pills in their containers. If the medicine is life-sustaining, the pills are dry and no replacement is available, FDA says they may be used until you can get a replacement.
- **Heat:** Medicines left in very hot places may lose strength. If something has changed in appearance, do not use it.
- **Freezing:** Some liquids and injectable medicines are damaged by freezing. Check the label.
- **Expired medicines:** These may not work as well. Replace them as soon as you can.

Ask a pharmacist when you can. They can tell you whether a particular medicine is still safe and effective.

## Medical devices that need power

People who use oxygen concentrators, CPAP machines, home dialysis, power wheelchairs or infusion pumps need a plan for power cuts.

- Know how long backup batteries last, and keep them charged.
- Ask your supplier or doctor what to do if the power is out for a long time.
- Some utilities keep a list of customers who rely on medical equipment. Ask to be added.
- Plan where you will go if power is not restored before batteries run out, such as a hospital or a shelter with power.
- Keep oxygen well away from candles, stoves and any flame.

See the Power Outage guide for more on medical equipment during blackouts.
