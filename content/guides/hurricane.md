Hurricanes bring dangerous wind, heavy rain, flooding and storm surge, and their effects can reach far inland. You usually get several days of warning, so the time before landfall is when your choices matter most.

## Watches, warnings and the first steps

- A **hurricane watch** means hurricane conditions are possible in your area, usually within **48 hours**.
- A **hurricane warning** means hurricane conditions are expected, usually within **36 hours**. Finish your preparations and leave if you are told to.
- A **storm surge warning** means there is a danger of life-threatening flooding from rising seawater.

When a watch is issued:

1. Listen to a battery or hand-crank radio or NOAA Weather Radio for updates.
2. Find out if you live in an **evacuation zone**, and know your route out.
3. Charge phones and power banks. Get some cash.
4. Fill your vehicle's fuel tank.
5. Refill prescriptions and pack medicines.
6. Make a plan for pets.

> **Warning:** Always follow evacuation orders from local officials. Leave early, before roads become crowded or flooded.

## Preparing your home and supplies

### Supplies

Have enough for at least several days, and ideally two weeks: water, food that needs no cooking, medicines, first aid supplies, flashlights, batteries, a radio, hygiene supplies and copies of important documents in a waterproof bag. The Emergency Kit guide has a full list.

### Your home

- Bring in or tie down outdoor furniture, bins, grills and anything that wind could throw.
- Cover windows with storm shutters or pre-cut exterior plywood. Tape does not stop glass from breaking.
- Clear gutters and drains.
- Move valuables and important papers to a higher floor if flooding is possible.
- Set the fridge and freezer to their coldest settings to keep food cold longer if the power goes out.
- Fill clean containers with drinking water.

## Evacuate or shelter in place

**Evacuate** if:

- Local officials tell you to leave
- You live in a storm surge or flood-prone area
- You live in a **mobile home** or other temporary structure. These are not safe in hurricane winds, even if tied down
- You live in a high-rise, where winds are stronger on upper floors
- You depend on power for medical equipment and have no backup

You may not need to travel far. Going tens of miles inland, out of the surge zone, can be enough. Go to friends or family on high ground, a hotel or a public shelter.

**Shelter in place** only if officials have not ordered evacuation and your home is sturdy and outside flood and surge areas. Once the storm arrives, it is usually too late to leave safely.

## Storm surge: the greatest danger

Storm surge is seawater pushed onshore by the storm's winds. It is historically the leading cause of death in hurricanes.

- Surge can raise water levels by many feet in a short time and flood areas miles inland, especially along bays and rivers.
- It comes with powerful waves that can destroy buildings.
- You cannot outrun it once it begins, and roads may flood before the strongest winds arrive.

If you are in a storm surge evacuation zone and are told to leave, **leave**. Do not stay to protect property.

Remember that floodwater from heavy rain is also deadly, even far from the coast. Never walk or drive through floodwater. Just 6 inches (15 cm) of moving water can knock you down. See the Floods guide.

## Sheltering during the storm

If you are sheltering at home:

- Stay indoors and **away from windows, skylights and glass doors**.
- Go to a small, windowless **interior room, closet or hallway on the lowest floor** that is not likely to flood.
- If wind becomes extreme, take cover under a sturdy table or other strong object.
- Keep your radio, flashlight, shoes and phone with you.
- If water rises inside the house, go to the highest level. **Do not climb into a closed attic**, where you could be trapped. Go onto the roof only if necessary to signal for help.

### Beware the calm of the eye

If the wind suddenly drops, you may be in the eye of the storm. **Stay inside.** The wind will return, often very quickly and from the opposite direction.

> **Warning:** Use flashlights, not candles. Never use a generator, grill or camp stove indoors.

## After the storm

Wait until officials say it is safe before going outside or returning home.

- **Stay away from floodwater and downed power lines.** Assume every wire is live.
- Watch for weakened trees, damaged roofs and loose debris.
- If you smell gas or hear hissing, leave and report it from a safe place.
- Do not enter a home that has structural damage until it has been checked.
- Wear sturdy boots, gloves and long sleeves during clean-up. Work with a partner and take breaks in the shade.
- Throw away food that touched floodwater or that has been above 40°F (4°C) for 2 hours or more. See the Food guide.
- Follow any boil-water advisory.
- Take photos of damage before cleaning, for insurance.

Heat after a hurricane is a serious risk when the power is out. See the Extreme Heat guide.

## Carbon monoxide after hurricanes

Carbon monoxide poisoning from generators kills people after almost every major hurricane.

- **Never use a generator, grill, camp stove or charcoal indoors**, including in a garage, basement or porch, even with doors and windows open.
- Place generators **at least 20 feet (6 metres)** from the home, away from windows, doors and vents.
- Use battery-powered CO alarms.

Symptoms include headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion. If anyone has symptoms or an alarm sounds, get everyone outside into fresh air immediately and call for help. See the Power Outage and First Aid guides.
