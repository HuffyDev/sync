A power outage is usually an inconvenience, but it can become dangerous through carbon monoxide, fire, spoiled food or medical equipment that stops working. This guide covers the most important steps in a sensible order.

## First hour checklist

Work through these steps calmly. Most of them take only a minute.

1. Check that everyone in the home is safe. Anyone who relies on powered medical equipment comes first (see the section below).
2. Get flashlights or battery lanterns out. Avoid candles if you can.
3. Keep the fridge and freezer doors closed.
4. Check whether the outage is just your home. Look at your breaker panel or fuse box and at your neighbours' lights.
5. Unplug sensitive electronics and appliances that were running, such as computers, TVs and microwaves. Leave one lamp switched on so you know when power returns.
6. Turn on a battery or hand-crank radio for local news and official instructions.
7. Put your phone into low power mode and save the battery for important calls and messages.
8. Fill containers with water if you are on a well with an electric pump, or if officials warn that water service may be affected.
9. Think ahead: if it is very hot or very cold, decide now where you would go if the outage lasts a long time.

> **Warning:** Stay away from downed power lines. Treat every fallen line as live and keep well back from it.

## Keep the fridge and freezer closed

The single most useful thing you can do for your food is keep the doors shut. According to USDA guidance:

- A closed refrigerator keeps food cold for about **4 hours**.
- A full freezer holds its temperature for about **48 hours**. A half-full freezer holds for about **24 hours**.

Every time you open the door, cold air escapes and that time gets shorter. Decide what you need before opening, take it out quickly and close the door.

If you expect a long outage, ice, frozen water bottles or dry ice can keep things cold longer. Handle dry ice with gloves or tongs, never touch it with bare skin, and keep the area ventilated.

An appliance thermometer in the fridge and freezer tells you for certain whether food stayed safe. Perishable food that has been above **40°F (4°C) for 2 hours or more** should be thrown out. The Food guide has full details on what to keep and what to discard.

## Generator safety and carbon monoxide

Generators produce carbon monoxide (CO), a poisonous gas you cannot see or smell. CO from generators kills people every year during outages, often while they sleep.

- **Never run a generator indoors**, including in a garage, basement, shed, crawl space or porch, even with doors and windows open.
- Place it outside, **at least 20 feet (6 metres) away from windows, doors and vents** (CDC guidance), so fumes cannot drift inside.
- Point the exhaust away from your home and your neighbours' homes.
- Keep it dry. Do not run it in rain or on wet ground unless it is under an open, canopy-like cover.
- Turn it off and let it cool before refuelling. Store fuel outside living areas in approved containers.
- Plug appliances directly into the generator with heavy-duty outdoor extension cords.
- **Never plug a generator into a wall outlet** ("backfeeding"). It can electrocute utility workers and neighbours. Only a licensed electrician should connect a generator to home wiring, using a proper transfer switch.

> **Warning:** If anyone feels dizzy, sick, weak or headachy while a generator or fuel-burning device is running, get everyone into fresh air immediately and call for help.

## Carbon monoxide alarms

A working CO alarm is your best protection against invisible poisoning, especially during outages when people use generators, heaters and camp stoves.

- Install battery-powered or battery-backup CO alarms on each level of the home and outside sleeping areas.
- Plug-in alarms without a battery backup will not work during a blackout.
- Test alarms and replace batteries according to the manufacturer's instructions.
- If a CO alarm sounds, do not search for the source. Get everyone, including pets, outside into fresh air right away. Call emergency services from outside.
- Do not go back inside until emergency responders say it is safe.

Common symptoms of CO poisoning are headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach or vomiting, chest pain and confusion. People who are asleep or drinking alcohol can die before they notice symptoms. See the First Aid guide for more.

## Light without fire

Flashlights and battery lanterns are far safer than candles. Candle fires rise during power outages.

- Keep a flashlight within reach for each person, plus spare batteries.
- Headlamps leave your hands free for cooking, first aid or caring for children.
- If you must use candles, put them in sturdy holders on a stable surface, at least 12 inches (30 cm) from anything that can burn.
- Never leave a candle burning in an empty room or while you sleep.
- Keep candles and matches away from children and pets.
- Do not use candles or open flames when checking fuel tanks, gas appliances or in rooms where you might smell gas.

> **Warning:** If you smell gas, do not light anything or flip switches. Leave the building and call the gas company or emergency services from outside.

## Medical devices and medicines that need refrigeration

People who depend on electricity for health need a plan before the battery runs out.

- **Powered medical devices** (oxygen concentrators, ventilators, CPAP, home dialysis, power wheelchairs, infusion pumps): check how long backup batteries last. Contact your equipment supplier or doctor for advice. If power will not return in time, go to a hospital, shelter or other place with power before the battery is empty.
- If anyone needs oxygen, keep oxygen and tanks well away from candles, stoves and any flame.
- **Medicines that need refrigeration**, such as insulin and some liquid antibiotics: check the label or package insert. Many types of insulin can be kept at room temperature for a limited number of days, but the exact time and temperature range differ by product.
- Do not use medicine that looks unusual (cloudy when it should be clear, clumped or discoloured).
- If a refrigerated medicine may have been damaged and it is life-sustaining, keep using it until you can get a replacement, then speak with a pharmacist or doctor as soon as you can, as advised by the US FDA.
- Keep a written list of medicines, doses and devices with you in case you need to leave.

## Protect electronics from surges

Power can come back with a surge or flicker on and off several times, which can damage electronics.

- Unplug computers, TVs, game consoles, microwaves and other sensitive equipment during the outage.
- Turn off or unplug appliances that were running when the power went out, such as space heaters, ovens, irons and hair tools, so they do not switch back on unattended.
- Leave **one lamp** switched on so you know when power returns.
- Surge protectors help, but unplugging is the most reliable protection.
- Keep laptops, power banks and phones charged whenever power is available, and use them sparingly when it is not.

## When the power comes back

Restore things gradually, and check that the house is safe.

1. Wait a few minutes after power returns before plugging things back in.
2. Plug in and turn on appliances one at a time to avoid overloading circuits.
3. Check the fridge and freezer temperatures before deciding what food to keep. Use the Food guide.
4. Reset clocks, alarms, and any medical or security devices.
5. If you see sparks, smell burning or see damaged wiring, switch off the main breaker and call an electrician.
6. If your home was flooded, do not turn on power until an electrician has checked it.
7. Replace used batteries, refill water containers and restock your kit so you are ready next time.
