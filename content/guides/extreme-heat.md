Extreme heat kills more people in the US than most other weather events, and the danger rises sharply when the power is out and air conditioning stops. This guide covers how to stay cool, who to watch closely and when heat becomes an emergency.

## The most important rules

- **Stay out of the sun** and limit activity during the hottest part of the day, usually late morning to late afternoon.
- **Drink water regularly**, even before you feel thirsty.
- Spend time in the coolest place you can reach. If a cooling centre, library or other cool public building is open, go there for at least a few hours a day.
- Wear loose, lightweight, light-coloured clothing.
- **Never leave a child, an older adult or a pet in a parked vehicle**, even for a few minutes with the windows open.
- Check on people at high risk at least twice a day.
- Learn the signs of heat exhaustion and heat stroke, and act early.

> **Warning:** Heat stroke can kill or cause lasting harm. If someone is confused, has a very high temperature or passes out, treat it as an emergency.

## Who is at highest risk

Anyone can get sick from heat, but some people are in much more danger:

- **Adults 65 and older**, especially those who live alone
- **Babies and young children**
- People with heart disease, high blood pressure, diabetes, lung disease or kidney disease
- People with mental illness or dementia
- People who are pregnant
- People taking certain medicines, such as some for blood pressure, heart conditions or mental health. Do not stop a medicine on your own. Ask a doctor or pharmacist.
- People who work or exercise outdoors
- People with obesity or who are ill with fever or diarrhoea
- People who cannot easily move, or who rely on others for care

Older adults may not feel thirst or heat in the usual way. Remind them to drink and help them move to a cooler place.

## Keeping cool without power

Without air conditioning, aim to keep heat out by day and let it out at night.

- **Close curtains, blinds and shutters** on windows that get sun. Light-coloured coverings help most.
- Keep windows closed during the hottest hours if it is hotter outside than inside. Open them in the evening and at night when the air outside is cooler.
- Stay on the **lowest floor**. Upper floors and attics get much hotter.
- Take **cool showers or baths**, or sponge down with cool water. Wet cloths on the neck, wrists and armpits also help.
- Avoid using the oven or stove. Eat light, cool meals.
- Rest during the heat of the day. Do heavy tasks in the early morning.

A battery fan can make you feel more comfortable, but when the temperature is in the high 90s °F (around 35°C or more), **a fan alone will not prevent heat illness**. Cooling the body with water and finding a cooler place matter more.

## Drinking enough

- Drink water regularly through the day. Do not wait until you are thirsty.
- Avoid alcohol and very sugary drinks. They can make you lose more body fluid.
- If you sweat a lot, also eat normal meals or snacks to replace lost salt, or use a sports drink or oral rehydration solution.
- If you are working outside, CDC suggests about **1 cup (8 ounces, about 240 ml) every 15 to 20 minutes**. Do not drink more than about **48 ounces (1.5 quarts, about 1.4 litres) in an hour**, because too much water can also be dangerous.
- If a doctor limits how much fluid you drink, or you take water pills, ask how much to drink when it is hot.

Light yellow urine is a sign you are drinking enough. Dark urine or very little urine means you need more fluid. The Water guide explains how to make water safe to drink.

## Signs of heat illness

### Heat exhaustion

Signs include heavy sweating, cold, pale and clammy skin, a fast and weak pulse, nausea or vomiting, muscle cramps, tiredness or weakness, dizziness, headache and fainting.

1. Move the person to a cooler place.
2. Loosen tight clothing.
3. Put cool, wet cloths on the body or have them take a cool bath.
4. Give sips of water.
5. Get medical help if they are vomiting, if symptoms get worse, or if symptoms last longer than 1 hour.

### Heat stroke

Signs include a body temperature of **103°F (39.4°C) or higher**, hot, red, dry or damp skin, a fast and strong pulse, headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion and passing out.

1. **Call 911 or emergency services** if any phone works.
2. Move the person to a cooler place.
3. Lower their temperature with cool cloths or a cool bath.
4. **Do not give them anything to drink.**

See the First Aid guide for more detail.

## Children, pets and vehicles

- The inside of a parked car can heat up very quickly, even on a mild day and with windows cracked open. Never leave anyone inside, and check the back seat every time you leave the car.
- Dress babies and children in light, loose clothing and keep them in the shade.
- Give children water often, and keep breastfeeding babies on demand.
- Watch children for fussiness, unusual sleepiness, fewer wet diapers or dark urine.

### Pets

- Give pets plenty of fresh water and shade, and bring them inside if you can.
- Walk dogs in the early morning or evening. Hot pavement can burn their paws.
- Heavy panting, drooling, weakness or collapse can mean a pet is overheating. Move it to a cooler place and wet it with cool, not ice-cold, water.

## Checking on others

Many deaths in heat waves are among people who live alone and are not checked on. If it is safe, visit older neighbours and people at high risk at least twice a day.

When you visit, ask:

- Is the home very hot? Is there a cooler room or place they can go?
- Are they drinking enough water?
- Do they have their medicines, and are the medicines being stored somewhere cool?
- Do they show any signs of heat illness, such as confusion, dizziness or weakness?

Help them close curtains, find a cooler spot, cool down with water, or get to a cooling centre. If someone seems confused or very unwell, get medical help right away.

For medicines that can be damaged by heat, see the Medications guide.
