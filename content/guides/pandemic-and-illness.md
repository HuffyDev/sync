When clinics are overwhelmed or hard to reach, many people with infections will be cared for at home. This guide covers how to care for a sick person safely and protect everyone else in the household.

## When to seek medical care urgently

Get emergency help right away, if any phone works or help can be reached, for anyone with:

- **Trouble breathing** or shortness of breath at rest
- **Pain or pressure in the chest** that does not go away
- **New confusion**, or being unable to wake up or stay awake
- **Pale, grey or blue-coloured** skin, lips or nail beds
- A **seizure**
- Severe or constant vomiting, or signs of serious dehydration such as very little urine
- A **stiff neck with fever**
- Symptoms that **get better and then return worse**, with fever and a worse cough

### Babies and young children

- Any baby **under 3 months** with a temperature of **100.4°F (38°C) or higher** needs medical care right away.
- Get help quickly for a child who breathes fast or with effort, is unusually sleepy or hard to wake, will not drink, or has no wet diaper for many hours.

> **Warning:** If you are unsure whether someone is seriously ill, it is safer to get help early.

## Stopping germs from spreading

Simple habits prevent many infections.

- **Wash hands with soap and water for at least 20 seconds**, especially after using the toilet, before preparing food or eating, after caring for a sick person and after coughing or blowing your nose.
- If soap and water are not available, use **hand sanitizer with at least 60% alcohol**. It does not work as well on visibly dirty hands. The Sanitation guide has ways to wash hands without running water.
- **Cover coughs and sneezes** with a tissue or your elbow. Throw tissues away and wash your hands.
- Avoid touching your eyes, nose and mouth.
- **Clean and disinfect** often-touched surfaces daily, such as door handles, taps, tables, toilets and phones.
- Bring in fresh air. Open windows when weather allows.
- Stay home when you are sick, and keep sick children home.

During an outbreak, follow advice from public health officials about masks, gatherings and vaccination.

## Isolating a sick person at home

If someone has a contagious illness, keep them apart from others as much as possible.

- Put the sick person in a **separate room** with the door closed. If possible, give them their own bathroom.
- If they must share a bathroom, clean it after they use it.
- Choose **one caregiver**. If possible, this should not be someone at higher risk, such as an older adult, a pregnant person or someone with a long-term illness.
- The sick person should wear a well-fitting mask when others are in the room, if they can. The caregiver should wear one too.
- Keep the room well aired. Open a window if the weather allows.
- Do not share dishes, cups, towels or bedding. Wash dishes with hot, soapy water.
- Keep a bin lined with a bag in the room for tissues and waste.
- The sick person should eat in their room if possible.
- Keep visitors away.

Many illnesses spread before or soon after symptoms start, so stay alert for symptoms in others in the household.

## Caring for someone with a fever

Fever is the body's normal response to infection. The goal is comfort and enough fluids.

- Encourage **rest** and plenty of **fluids**.
- Dress them in light clothing and use a light blanket. Do not bundle them up.
- A lukewarm sponge bath can help comfort. Do not use ice or rubbing alcohol.
- Fever-reducing medicines such as **acetaminophen (paracetamol)** or **ibuprofen** can help. Follow the label exactly. For children, use the dose for their weight or age and the measuring device that comes with the medicine.
- Do not give more than one product containing the same medicine. Many cold and flu products already contain acetaminophen.

### Keep a simple record

Write down the time, temperature, medicines given and how much the person drinks and urinates. This helps you notice changes and helps any health worker who sees them later.

> **Warning:** Never give aspirin to children or teenagers who have a fever or viral illness. It can cause Reye's syndrome, a rare but serious illness.

## Fluids, vomiting and diarrhoea

Fever, vomiting and diarrhoea all cause the body to lose water. Dehydration is a common and serious complication, especially in babies, young children and older adults.

- Give **small, frequent sips** of safe water or other fluids.
- If the person has diarrhoea or is vomiting, **oral rehydration solution** replaces both water and salts better than plain water. Use packaged oral rehydration salts if you have them. The First Aid guide has a home recipe.
- Keep breastfeeding babies throughout illness.
- Start normal, easy-to-digest food again as soon as the person feels able to eat.

Watch for signs of dehydration: thirst, dry mouth, little or dark urine, dizziness and tiredness. In babies, look for fewer wet diapers, no tears when crying and unusual sleepiness.

Anyone with diarrhoea should wash their hands carefully after using the toilet, and should not prepare food for others. See the Sanitation guide for safe toilet use and cleaning.

## Cleaning, laundry and waste

- Wear disposable gloves if you have them when cleaning the sick person's room or handling their laundry and waste. Wash your hands after taking them off.
- **Clean surfaces first** with soap and water, then disinfect them. The Sanitation guide has the CDC bleach recipe.
- Do not shake dirty laundry, to avoid spreading germs into the air.
- Wash laundry using the warmest water suitable for the fabric, and dry it completely. Laundry from a sick person can be washed with other items.
- Put tissues, masks and other waste in a lined bin. Tie the bag closed before removing it.
- Clean and disinfect anything soiled with vomit or diarrhoea straight away.

> **Warning:** Never mix bleach with ammonia or any other cleaner. Mixing can release toxic gases.

## Protecting people at higher risk

Some people are more likely to become seriously ill from infections:

- Adults 65 and older
- Babies and young children
- People who are pregnant
- People with long-term conditions such as heart disease, lung disease, diabetes or kidney disease
- People with weakened immune systems

To protect them:

- Keep them away from anyone who is sick, if possible in a different part of the home.
- Make sure they have enough of their regular medicines. See the Medications guide.
- Check on them often for new symptoms, and seek care earlier for them than you would for others.
- Keep up good hand hygiene and fresh air throughout the home.
