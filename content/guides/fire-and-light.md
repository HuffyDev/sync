Home fires become more common during power outages, when people turn to candles, fuel lamps and improvised heating, and fire crews may be slow to arrive. This guide covers safe light, preventing fires and what to do if one starts.

## Safe lighting in a blackout

The safest light is **battery-powered**. Use it instead of candles whenever you can.

- **Flashlights and headlamps** are best for moving around. A headlamp keeps your hands free.
- **LED lanterns** light a whole room and use little battery power.
- **Glow sticks** are safe for children and for marking doorways and stairs.
- Solar garden lights can be charged outside by day and brought in at night.

Keep a flashlight by every bed and in each main room, and store plenty of spare batteries. LED bulbs make batteries last much longer than older bulbs.

### Fuel-burning lanterns

Kerosene, propane and gas camping lanterns produce carbon monoxide and can start fires.

- Use them only with good ventilation, and never in a room where people sleep.
- Refuel them **outdoors**, only after they have cooled.
- Use only the fuel the manufacturer specifies.

> **Warning:** Never use a camp stove, grill or charcoal for light or heat indoors. They can kill with carbon monoxide.

## If you must use candles

Candles are a common cause of home fires during power outages. If you have no other light:

- Keep candles **at least 12 inches (30 cm)** from anything that can burn, such as curtains, bedding, paper and furniture.
- Use **sturdy holders** that will not tip over, on a flat, uncluttered surface.
- **Never leave a candle burning unattended.** Blow it out when you leave the room or go to sleep.
- Keep candles out of reach of children and pets.
- Do not carry a lit candle. Use a flashlight to move around.
- Do not use a candle to look inside closets, cupboards or fuel containers.
- Never use candles near medical oxygen.
- Put out candles before they burn down to within about 2 inches (5 cm) of the holder.

Candles in glass jars or enclosed holders are safer than open tapers. Battery candles are safer still.

## Smoke alarms and carbon monoxide alarms

Working smoke alarms cut the risk of dying in a home fire by about half. During a power outage, hard-wired alarms only work if they have a **battery backup**.

- Install smoke alarms **inside each bedroom**, **outside each sleeping area** and on **every level** of the home, including the basement.
- **Test alarms every month** by pressing the test button.
- Replace batteries at least once a year, or as soon as an alarm chirps for a low battery, unless it has a sealed 10-year battery.
- **Replace smoke alarms every 10 years.**
- Never remove batteries to use them elsewhere.

### Carbon monoxide alarms

Put CO alarms outside each sleeping area and on every level. Use battery-powered alarms or ones with battery backup. If a CO alarm sounds, get everyone outside into fresh air at once and call for help. See the Power Outage guide.

## Using a fire extinguisher: PASS

Only try to put out a fire if **all** of these are true:

- The fire is **small and contained**, such as in a wastebasket or a pan.
- Everyone else is leaving, and someone is calling for help if possible.
- The room is not filling with smoke.
- You have a **clear way out behind you**.
- You have the right extinguisher and know how to use it.

Stand several feet away, with your back to an exit, and remember **PASS**:

1. **Pull** the pin.
2. **Aim** low, at the base of the fire, not at the flames.
3. **Squeeze** the handle slowly and evenly.
4. **Sweep** the nozzle from side to side across the base of the fire.

If the fire does not go out quickly, or starts to spread, **get out**. A multipurpose extinguisher labelled **ABC** works on most home fires.

> **Warning:** Never throw water on a grease or cooking oil fire. Cover the pan with a lid, turn off the heat and leave the lid on.

## If a fire starts: get out, stay out

A small fire can fill a home with deadly smoke in a few minutes. Your goal is to get everyone out.

1. **Shout "Fire!"** to warn everyone.
2. **Get low** and crawl under the smoke. The cleaner air is near the floor.
3. Before opening a door, **feel the door and handle with the back of your hand**. If it is hot, use another way out.
4. **Close doors behind you** to slow the fire.
5. Go to your agreed meeting place outside.
6. **Call for help from outside.**
7. **Never go back inside** a burning building.

If your clothes catch fire: **Stop, Drop and Roll**. Stop where you are, drop to the ground, cover your face with your hands and roll over and over until the flames are out. Then cool the burn with cool water. See the First Aid guide.

### Plan ahead

Know two ways out of every room, and make sure windows and doors open easily. Practise your escape plan with everyone at home, including at night.

## Lighting a fire outdoors safely

An outdoor fire can provide warmth, light and a way to cook or boil water. Light one only when it is safe and allowed.

- **Check for fire bans** and do not light fires in dry, windy weather.
- Use an existing **fire ring or pit** if there is one.
- Choose a spot **at least 15 feet (about 5 metres)** from tents, buildings, shrubs, trees and other things that can burn. Watch for overhanging branches.
- Clear an area about **10 feet (3 metres) across** down to bare soil.
- Keep a **bucket of water and a shovel** close by before you start.
- Keep the fire small.

### Building it

1. Start with a small pile of **tinder**, such as dry grass, paper or wood shavings.
2. Add **kindling**: small, dry sticks.
3. Light the tinder. Once the kindling burns well, add larger pieces of dry wood one at a time.

Never use gasoline or other fuels to start or speed up a fire.

## Putting an outdoor fire out completely

Never leave a fire unattended, even for a short time. Put it out fully before you leave or sleep.

1. **Drown** the fire with lots of water. Pour until the hissing stops.
2. **Stir** the ashes and embers with a shovel, scraping any burnt wood.
3. **Drown** it again.
4. **Feel** for heat by holding the back of your hand close to the ashes. Do not touch them directly.

If it is too hot to touch, it is too hot to leave. Repeat until everything is cold. If you have no water, mix in dirt or sand and stir until cold, but do not simply bury the fire, as it can keep smouldering.

### Cooking and charcoal

Cook outdoors only, away from the house. Never bring a grill or charcoal indoors, even after you have finished cooking, as it can still give off carbon monoxide. Let ashes cool fully and store them in a metal container away from the house.
