Wildfires can move fast and change direction with the wind, and their smoke can harm health hundreds of miles away. This guide covers evacuation, protecting yourself from smoke and staying safe after the fire.

## Evacuate early

- **Leave immediately if officials tell you to.** Do not wait to see the flames.
- If you feel unsafe, leave even before an order. Leaving early gives you more route choices and keeps roads clear for firefighters.
- Know at least two ways out of your neighbourhood. Fire can block one.
- Keep your vehicle fuelled and parked facing out, with your go-bag ready.
- Drive with headlights on and windows closed. Watch for people, animals and fire vehicles.
- Take pets with you.

### If you are trapped

- Call 911 or emergency services if any phone works, and give your location.
- Turn on lights so rescuers can find you in the smoke.
- Stay away from areas with lots of dry grass, brush and trees.

> **Warning:** Wildfire can spread faster than you can run or drive on a blocked road. When in doubt, get out early.

## Your wildfire go-bag

Keep a bag packed during fire season so you can leave in minutes. In addition to the basics in the Emergency Kit guide, include:

- **N95 respirators** for each person old enough to wear one
- Water and food that needs no cooking
- Prescription medicines, glasses and a copy of your medication list
- Phone, chargers and a power bank
- A battery or hand-crank radio and a flashlight
- Copies of important documents and some cash
- Long-sleeved shirts, long trousers and sturdy shoes made of cotton or wool, which are less likely to melt than synthetic fabrics
- Pet food, leashes, carriers and medicines
- Eye drops and a change of clothes

## If you have time before leaving

Only do these things if there is time and officials have not told you to leave immediately:

- Close all windows and doors, but leave them unlocked so firefighters can get in.
- Take down light curtains and move furniture away from windows and doors, to the centre of the room.
- Bring flammable outdoor furniture, doormats and cushions inside.
- Turn off gas at the meter or propane tank if officials advise it. Only a professional should turn it back on.
- Turn off air conditioning so it does not draw in smoke.
- Leave outside and inside lights on so firefighters can see your home through the smoke.
- Connect garden hoses to outdoor taps where firefighters can find them.

## Smoke and your health

Wildfire smoke contains tiny particles that get deep into the lungs and even the bloodstream.

### Who is most at risk

- People with heart or lung disease, including asthma and COPD
- Older adults
- Children and teenagers
- People who are pregnant
- Outdoor workers

### Signs smoke is affecting you

Coughing, a scratchy throat, stinging eyes, a runny nose, headaches, tiredness and trouble breathing are common. **Get medical help** for chest pain, a fast heartbeat, severe shortness of breath or extreme tiredness. People with asthma should keep their inhalers and action plan close.

### Reducing your exposure

- If you can see or smell smoke, **stay indoors** and keep windows and doors closed, unless it is too hot to stay safe. Heat can be dangerous too.
- Avoid heavy outdoor activity.
- If the air quality index (AQI) is available on a radio or app, values above 100 are unhealthy for sensitive groups, and above 150 are unhealthy for everyone.

## Masks: use N95 respirators, not cloth masks

**Cloth masks, surgical masks, bandanas and wet towels do not protect you from smoke particles.** They can give a false sense of safety.

A properly fitted **N95 or P100 respirator** can reduce the smoke you breathe.

- Choose one marked **N95 or P100** and approved by NIOSH. It should have **two straps** that go around the head.
- Pull both straps into place and press the metal nose piece to shape it to your nose.
- It must seal against the skin. Beards and facial hair stop it from sealing.
- Check the fit: breathe out hard. If air leaks around the edges, adjust it.
- Replace it when it becomes dirty, damaged or harder to breathe through.

Respirators are not made for young children and may not fit them. Keep children indoors in the cleanest air you can. People with heart or lung disease should ask a doctor before wearing one, as it makes breathing harder.

## Making a clean room

A clean room helps you breathe cleaner air indoors when there is smoke outside.

1. Choose a room big enough for everyone, with as few windows and doors as possible, such as a bedroom.
2. Close the windows and doors. Seal gaps if you can.
3. If you have power, run a **portable air cleaner with a HEPA filter** sized for the room. Run it on the highest setting you can tolerate.
4. If you have air conditioning, set it to recirculate and close the fresh-air intake.
5. Stay cool. Use fans and air conditioning to avoid overheating.

### Do not add pollution indoors

- Do not smoke, burn candles or incense, or use a fireplace.
- Avoid frying or broiling food and using gas stoves.
- Do not vacuum unless the vacuum has a HEPA filter, as it stirs up particles.

If you have no power, keep the room closed and as still as possible, and consider moving to a cleaner-air shelter or library if one is open.

## Returning home after a fire

Go home only when officials say it is safe.

- Watch for hot spots, smouldering debris, damaged trees and downed power lines.
- If you smell gas, leave and report it from a safe distance.
- Use flashlights, not candles.
- Follow any boil-water advisory. Fire can damage water systems.
- Throw away food that was exposed to heat, smoke or soot.

### Cleaning up ash

Ash can irritate skin, eyes and lungs, and may contain harmful substances.

- Wear an **N95 respirator**, gloves, goggles, long sleeves and long trousers.
- Lightly mist ash with water before cleaning to keep the dust down.
- Use damp cloths and mops. Do not use leaf blowers or dry sweeping, and only use a vacuum with a HEPA filter.
- Keep children and pets away from ash, and wash any skin that touches it.
